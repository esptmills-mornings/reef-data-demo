Across four independent events spanning 26 years, the global bleaching peak consistently follows the ONI peak by roughly 2–4 months. This is the kind of output the lag-alignment engine in the architecture doc is meant to surface automatically, per location, once real per-reef heat-stress data is plugged in — rather than requiring a domain expert to already know the relationship. Correlation across four events is suggestive, not proof of causation on its own — the underlying physical mechanism (basin-wide ocean heat content driving thermal stress) is well established in the coral science literature, which is what turns this from a coincidence into a real signal worth building on.

The clean "El Niño → bleaching" story holds for 1998, 2002, 2016, and 2024 — all El Niño years with high severity. But 2017, 2020, and 2022 break it: the GBR bleached severely during ENSO-neutral or La Niña conditions, when the classic teleconnection wouldn't predict trouble. This is exactly the kind of thing a single-variable dashboard would miss and a unified entity timeline surfaces automatically — the pattern shifted around 2016, consistent with rising baseline ocean temperature now driving bleaching on its own, independent of the ENSO cycle. A real platform would flag this shift rather than force-fit one lag number across 26 years of data. Layering in cyclone data confirms the two disturbance channels are genuinely independent at the GBR most of the time — three Category 5 cyclones (Larry 2006, Hamish 2009, Yasi 2011) hit in years with no mass bleaching at all — except 2017, where Cyclone Debbie struck the same year as the second consecutive bleaching event, compounding thermal and mechanical damage exactly the way Hurricane Mitch did at the Mesoamerican Reef in 1998. Same cross-source pattern, two different ocean basins.

Second entity — Mesoamerican Reef

A different reef system (Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras — world's 2nd largest barrier reef) tested against the same ONI signal. Its monitoring record is real but noticeably less complete than the GBR's — worth showing as-is rather than smoothing over, since uneven source coverage per entity is a real constraint any version of this platform has to represent honestly rather than hide.

Two things stand out. First, cross-basin confirmation: the Mesoamerican Reef's 2017 event also happened at ONI +0.35 — essentially ENSO-neutral — the same non-El-Niño bleaching pattern seen at the GBR that year. Two independent reef systems bleaching outside the classic El Niño trigger, in the same year, is stronger evidence for a rising-baseline-temperature effect than either data point alone. Second, the entity comparison surfaces a real data-quality problem: MAR's monitoring network (Healthy Reefs Initiative, active since 2015) is younger and less funded than the GBR's, so 1998, 2005, 2010, and 2017 have only qualitative or missing severity data. A platform built on this architecture needs to represent that confidence gap explicitly — exactly what the "provenance/confidence" open question in the architecture doc was flagging — rather than quietly treating a well-instrumented reef and a sparsely-instrumented one as equally reliable.

Third data source — tropical cyclones

Hurricane Mitch complicated the 1998 MAR bleaching read — it's a real, separate disturbance channel (mechanical storm damage) layered on top of thermal stress, not a substitute for it. Adding it as its own tracked source, the way the architecture doc's entity timeline is meant to hold multiple simultaneous stressor types rather than a single variable.

Two of the region's most destructive storms — Mitch (1998) and Wilma (2005) — land in the exact two years where MAR bleaching severity couldn't be cleanly quantified. That's very unlikely to be coincidence: major hurricanes disrupt the survey teams and timing needed to measure bleaching independently of storm damage, and the mechanical destruction itself can mask or compound thermal bleaching signs on the reef. This is a concrete example of why an entity's data-confidence flag (from the previous panel) needs to be able to point to a *reason* — "storm disruption," not just "no data" — which is exactly the kind of explanation a unified timeline can surface automatically by checking what else was happening to that location at the same time, instead of a person having to already know to go looking for Hurricane Mitch.
